SOCIAL REFERENT SEEDING · THE BELAY LINE
Paluck Model — adolescent norms are set by informal peer influencers, not appointed leaders.
1
Identification via Network Mapping
short peer survey · every subculture, not just top students
Instead of selecting top academic or student-council leaders, a short peer survey identifies high-degree social nodes across every subculture — including students frequently linked to minor behavioral friction.
2
Grassroots Co-Design
cross-clique cohorts
Referents are brought together in cross-clique cohorts to design public interventions, establish micro-rituals, and normalize speaking out against harassment.
3
Overcoming Pluralistic Ignorance
visible peer modeling
When influential peers visibly model upstander behavior, the perceived social cost of intervening drops across the whole student body.
The evidence — and where it already meets EMS

Paluck, Shepherd & Aronow (2016, PNAS) ran this exact model across 56 New Jersey middle schools and 24,191 students: randomly selected seed groups of 20–32 students publicly took a stance against everyday conflict. Disciplinary reports fell 30% over one year — and the effect grew larger when the seed group had more high-network-centrality “social referents” rather than officially appointed leaders.

EMS already has the seed-group infrastructure: the Mountaineer Leadership Corps (Generals, Captains, Ambassadors, Delegates, Vanguard, Scouts, Stewards) spans exactly these cross-clique, high-visibility roles. A short, anonymous peer-nomination survey (“who would you go to if…”) could surface additional referents outside the Corps roster who carry more day-to-day social weight than a title predicts.
